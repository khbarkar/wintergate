01 / Mechanic

One verb, twenty-four doors

Portal's real trick was not portals — it was that the whole game ran on a single verb a player understood in ten seconds, then spent eight hours discovering. This needs the same shape, and it cannot be portals.

Borrow
& Lend

Everything in a diorama carries visible properties — small brass-tagged words clipped to the object: HEAVY, LOUD, FLIGHT, SHARP, SWEET, YOUNGEST, ASLEEP, GOLDEN, FIRST.

You cannot command anything. You can only borrow one property off a thing and lend it to another. Objects hold a limited number of properties, the world obeys them literally and instantly, and nothing else in the diorama changes unless the properties say it does.

Lend SWEET to a boulder and the bear hauls the boulder to its hoard. Borrow ASLEEP from a guard and the guard is awake — and something else is now sleeping. Borrow YOUNGEST from the third brother and the fairy-tale rule "the youngest always wins" picks someone new.

Why it carries 24 days: the puzzle space is combinatorial, not content-hungry. Authoring a door means picking six objects, nine properties and four rules, not building a new mechanic. And a verb that is pick up a word, put it somewhere else maps to a mouse click and to a VR hand with no redesign — which is the whole Quest port.

One twist per world

The verb never changes. What changes is the system that reads the properties — so each world feels like a different game while sharing every line of code.

Norse · Prophecy

The Prophecy Board

A carved prophecy is posted and must come true, to the letter. You rearrange properties until the literal words are satisfied. Constraint satisfaction with a weather system.

1001 Nights · Wishes

Three Wishes

Wishes are granted with malicious literalism. Each wish permanently welds a property to a thing — irreversible moves in an otherwise undoable game. Spend them wrong, restart the beat.

Fairy tales · Rules

The Posted Rule

Every tale has one law the world enforces mechanically: the youngest always wins, never eat at a stranger's table, three tries, then it's over. You don't break the law. You change who it points at.

Animal kingdom · Instinct

Instinct Scripts

Animals run short visible loops — hunt, hoard, migrate, imitate. You can't give orders, only edit what the loop sees. The only world with live agents moving while you think.

Rotation, not blocks. Doors cycle Norse → Nights → Fairy tale → Animal, so every day feels like a new world and nobody gets six days of snow. Each world's six doors still form an arc, and doors 21–24 braid all four together.

02 / Puzzles

Logic you can read off the table

No systems to learn, no tutorials to sit through, no twitch. Every door states its rules on a wooden sign and then expects you to think. Here is the first beat of Door 1 — playable.

The seven authoring rules

  1. One verb per game, one twist per world. If a door needs a new mechanic explained, it is the wrong door.
  2. All information is visible. No hidden state, no pixel hunting, no "you had to try it to know". A player with a notebook must be able to solve it away from the screen.
  3. No timing, no reflexes, no precision. Mandatory for VR parity and for 11-year-olds.
  4. Never pun-locked. A puzzle may be funny in English; it may never be solvable only in English. Non-negotiable for localisation.
  5. Four to six beats per door, each 8–15 minutes, each introducing exactly one new property or rule, with a 10-minute finale that uses all of them at once.
  6. Undo everything except wishes. Failure should cost ten seconds, so experimentation is the cheapest way to think.
  7. One optional ★ beat per door (the "gilt" puzzle) for people who want the hard version. Never gates the next day.

The hint ladder — load-bearing, not a nicety

A calendar where day 12 blocks day 13 makes every hard-walled player a refund and a one-star review. Hasp is the pressure valve, and the sass makes taking help feel like a scene rather than a defeat.

Every door is machine-verified

Property puzzles are a finite state graph, so solvability is not a playtest question — it is a build step. The editor tool runs breadth-first search over the whole state space of each beat and fails the build if anything is off.

// DoorValidator — runs on every save and in CI
assert solutions.count > 0            // is it solvable at all
assert minMoves >= 3 && minMoves <= 9  // is it worth solving
assert uniqueSolutionPaths <= 4        // not brute-forceable
assert deadEndStates == 0              // no unwinnable state without undo
assert newPropertiesIntroduced == 1    // one idea per beat
report beatDifficulty = log2(stateSpace) / minMoves

That last line gives you a comparable difficulty number for all 96 beats, which is how you build a curve across 24 days instead of guessing.

03 / Story

The Department of Unfinished Stories

Four unrelated mythologies need one frame that earns the mixing and licenses the jokes. A bureaucracy is that frame — and it is funnier than a wizard.

Frame

Stories that lose their endings get filed. Somewhere behind the world there is a department that holds them — twenty-four drawers, each with a story stuck mid-sentence because its internal logic broke. Thor's hammer is missing and the prophecy that was meant to recover it no longer parses. A djinn granted a wish that contradicts itself and the paperwork is still smouldering. A wolf has been served an eviction notice by three pigs with a permit.

You are the new Keyholder, hired on a seasonal contract, 24 days. Your job is not to be a hero. Your job is to make each story logically consistent again so it can be refiled, which mostly means moving properties around until the prophecy is technically true and nobody can sue.

Hasp

Your only colleague is the brass hasp on the calendar lid. Forty years of service, extremely qualified, deeply unimpressed. He is Portal-shaped — disembodied, constant, funnier than you — but where GLaDOS was a scientist who wanted to kill you, Hasp is a civil servant who wants to go home. He comments on your logic, your taste, your shortcuts, and files grievances about your working hours.

Hasp, on the cheat code: "You typed the skeleton key. In front of me. I have been a lock for four decades and you did that in front of me. No, go ahead. Door's open. We'll discuss it in my review."

Hasp, on Door 9: "The gods bound the wolf with the sound of a cat's footfall and the beard of a woman. Procurement was a nightmare. Receipts, somehow, are fine."

Where it ends

Door 24 is the only door with every world's cast in one diorama: an audit. All four systems run at once — a prophecy, a wish, a posted rule, and four live instinct loops — and the department's actual secret is that the stories were never filed by accident. Two to three hours, no new mechanics, every property you have met since Door 1. Hasp gets a chair.

04 / Artwork

Storybook dioramas in a brass box

Four worlds in one game is an art budget disaster unless the framing device does the heavy lifting. The framing device: every door opens into a small lit stage you look into, never walk through.

Direction

Diorama-first solves four problems at once. Levels are small, so one artist can finish a world. Lighting is baked, so Quest holds 72 fps. The camera is outside the scene, so VR needs no locomotion and earns a Comfortable rating. And the player is a giant reaching into a toy theatre, which is the single most-screenshotted thing you can put in a trailer.

Style target

  • Stylised low-poly with hand-painted texture. Readable silhouettes over detail — a property tag must be legible at 1 m in a Quest headset.
  • Carved-wood and brass vernacular for everything the player touches (property tags, the Prophecy Board, Hasp), so the interface belongs to the box rather than floating over it.
  • Baked AO plus one warm key light per diorama. No real-time shadows, no post stack beyond colour grade and a soft vignette.
  • Palette does the world-building, not geometry: a shared prop kit is retextured per world, which is how 24 dioramas fit a two-artist team.

The voice

Target: a clever 12-year-old and the adult who bought it for them should laugh at different lines in the same sentence. That means dry understatement and bureaucratic deadpan, not gag density — and it means the jokes come from the mythology colliding with modern process, which never stops being funny and never needs a trademark.

Pop-culture references without the rot

Dated references and legal exposure are the same problem with the same fix: reference the archetype, never the property.

  • Not a named franchise — "the wizard school with the appalling staircase safety record".
  • Not a named platform — "a streaming service that cancelled the saga after two seasons".
  • Evergreen over current: office life, group chats, terms and conditions, IKEA-shaped flat-pack instructions, customer support, HR.
  • All of it in optional barks. Jokes live in idle lines, object inspections and Hasp's commentary — never in rules text, never required to solve. One flag turns the humour density down for localisation or for a classroom build.

The hard content rule for 12+: comedy of inconvenience, never cruelty. Fenrir gets bound with paperwork; the wolf gets evicted, not skinned. Fairy tales are the source of the darkest material here, so pick the versions where the punishment is bureaucratic.

Localisation

Budget transcreation, not translation — a translator paid per word will flatten every joke in the game. Brief them to re-write the gag for the locale, keep the rule text literal.

Platform specifics worth planning now

  • macOS: universal binary, hardened runtime, notarised in CI from day one. Retrofitting notarisation two weeks before launch is a classic self-inflicted wound.
  • Quest 2 floor: 72 fps, <90k tris and <1 ms CPU per diorama, fixed foveated rendering, everything baked. Design the budget on Quest 2 and Quest 3 is free.
  • Input abstraction from the first commit: every interaction is select a property, select a destination. Mouse, gamepad and hand-tracking all feed that one interface. Do this early or pay for it twice.
  • Press and accessibility builds: a launch argument that opens all 24 doors, because reviewers will not play twelve hours before writing.
07 / Gating

Locks, dates, and the skeleton key

The daily gate is the hook and the biggest review risk in the design. Gate on solving, offer dates as a mode, and make the cheat code a first-class feature instead of an apology.

Rules
  • Default: progression gate. Door N+1 opens when Door N is solved. Buy it in March, play it in March. This is the gate that ships on.
  • Optional: Calendar mode. On during December, off by default outside it — one door per real day, Hasp doing a daily cold open. This is the thing that makes it a calendar, so make it an opt-in ritual, not a wall.
  • Clock protection, not clock policing. Store the highest date ever seen and prefer Steam's server time when online, so setting the system clock forward just quietly does nothing. Never block a player who is offline or travelling.
  • Hasp's skip (20-minute hint ladder) means nobody is ever truly stuck behind a door.

Cheat codes — typed on the brass plate of the lid

CodeEffectHasp

Design the cheat in, don't bolt it on. Reward it with content rather than punishing it: every cheated door still counts for completion, and Hasp's escalating resentment is a real joke thread with a payoff on Door 24. Players who cheat ahead on day one still come back to solve the doors, because the solving is the game — but the ones who cannot get past Door 9 and have no key are the ones who refund.

Store shape

  • Door 1 free as the demo, permanently — it is a complete 40-minute puzzle and the best sales pitch available.
  • One achievement per door plus four ★ gilt achievements, so the achievement list is itself the calendar.
  • Launch mid-November so December's calendar window and the winter sale both land, with a Next Fest demo in October.
08 / Scope

The honest arithmetic

"A few hours each, twenty-four days" is a 60–70 hour puzzle game. That is a bigger game than most puzzle studios ship in three years, so here is the real shape of it.

Recommendation: ship Season 1, twelve doors, PC and Mac. Twelve doors is still an advent calendar (a half calendar is a known format), it proves the one verb across all four worlds three times over, and it gets the Door Editor and the VR input abstraction built and paid for. Quest port next, Season 2 the following November — at which point the second twelve doors cost roughly 40% of the first twelve because every tool and kit already exists.

The thing that kills this project if you let it: 24 bespoke mechanics. One verb, four readers, machine-verified beats. That is the whole production strategy.

First 90 days

  1. Weeks 1–4: Door Editor skeleton and the BFS validator. Prove the pipeline on paper puzzles with placeholder cubes.
  2. Weeks 5–8: Door 1 vertical slice, art-complete, PC + Mac, playable on Quest with programmer hands.
  3. Weeks 9–12: Doors 2–4 — one per world — to prove the four readers. Then playtest Door 1 with twelve 11–14 year olds and watch where they stop reading.
